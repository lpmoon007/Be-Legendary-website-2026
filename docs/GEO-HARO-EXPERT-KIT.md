## How to get picked (the rules that matter)
1. **Be fast.** Most queries close in 24–48h; the first good answers win. Answer the same day.
2. **Answer the exact question** in the first sentence — reporters skim.
3. **Be specific and quotable.** One sharp, concrete claim beats five hedged ones. A number or a named mechanism (the Flag Model, the feedback vacuum) earns the cite.
4. **Be self-contained.** The reporter should be able to paste your answer verbatim. 50–120 words is the sweet spot.
5. **No marketing.** No "at Be Legendary we…" pitch. The bio line carries the brand; the answer earns the trust.
6. **Give them a reason to link** — reference the framework or the research so the natural attribution includes belegendary.org.

## Response templates *(structure, then fill)*

**The structure that gets used:**
> [Direct answer in one sentence.] [A specific mechanism or example — name the Flag Model / the real dynamic.] [A concrete, quotable line the reporter can lift.]
>
> — James Carter, founder of Be Legendary (belegendary.org)

### Worked example 1 — *"How do you fix a dysfunctional leadership team?"*
> Start by finding the one discipline that broke first — not the symptoms. Dysfunctional teams usually have a single failed discipline (decisions don't stick, or feedback stopped flowing) and everything else is downstream. I call it finding the break point. Fix the one that's load-bearing and the "dysfunction" often resolves without a single trust exercise. Most teams skip the diagnosis and go straight to offsites — which is why the offsites don't stick.
>
> — James Carter, founder of Be Legendary (belegendary.org)

### Worked example 2 — *"Is more honesty/candor always good for a team?"*
> No — and this surprises people. Candor without a shared standard for *how* to disagree often raises the temperature without improving decisions; the team confuses conflict for progress. The fix isn't less honesty, it's a disagreement discipline: a known way to surface dissent and then commit. Candor is only useful once the team can metabolize it.
>
> — James Carter, founder of Be Legendary (belegendary.org)

### Worked example 3 — *"Why don't leadership offsites create lasting change?"*
> Because an offsite is an event, and execution is a habit. Two days of alignment can't survive fifty-one weeks with no cadence. What actually changes a team is a weekly rhythm that keeps the one rebuilt discipline alive — measured, not hoped for. If you can't put a number on whether the team is following through, the offsite was theater.
>
> — James Carter, founder of Be Legendary (belegendary.org)

### Worked example 4 — *"What's the biggest mistake new CEOs make with their team?"*
> They try to build trust directly, as if it's the starting input. Trust is the *output* of disciplines that hold — decisions that stick, commitments that are kept, feedback that flows. Chase trust first and you get forced vulnerability; build the disciplines first and trust shows up on its own. Reverse the order most leadership books teach.
>
> — James Carter, founder of Be Legendary (belegendary.org)

## A note on numbers in quotes
James can cite measured results (e.g. a team that saw a 268% lift, or a 209% measured lift at Champion Iron) — these make a quote far more pickable. **Confirm the figure and its context with James before it goes in a published quote**, same rule as the podcast one-sheet. When in doubt, lead with the mechanism (the Flag Model, the feedback vacuum) rather than a number.
